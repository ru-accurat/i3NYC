What the data shows

Eight findings from the profiles and indicators below. Every figure is linked to its source in the sections that follow.

  • Where fashion is sold, not made

    New York City has more than 130,000 fashion jobs and over $50B in direct sales, but only about 5,700 apparel manufacturing jobs, down 76% since 2007. The city's strengths are headquarters, buyers, media and schools.

  • Innovation is software sold to brands

    Of 48 technology companies profiled, 22 sell software or services to brands and retailers and 11 run marketplaces. New York's fashion customers are its incumbents, so most startups build for them rather than compete with them.

  • AI is where the new money goes

    7 of the 12 companies that raised in 2025–26 build on AI, including Vivrelle, Phia, Raspberry AI, SuperCircle and Doji. Rounds stay small: $339M in total, led by ShopMy's $70M at a $1.5B valuation.

  • Circularity is a real cohort

    22 companies have a sustainability or circular model, from rental (Vivrelle, $62M Series C) and resale (Pickle, Rebag) to textile sorting (SuperCircle) and biomaterials (Modern Meadow, Werewool, Kintra). US resale grew about four times faster than apparel retail in 2025.

  • Luxury is buying Fifth Avenue

    Kering ($963M), Prada ($835M for 720 and 724 Fifth) and Rolex ($250M headquarters) have committed about $2B to Fifth Avenue buildings. Upper Fifth rents are $2,000 per sq ft, third in the world after New Bond Street and Via Montenapoleone.

  • Italian luxury runs its US business from here

    11 Italian luxury groups and brands in this map run their American business from New York, including Prada, Armani, Zegna, Moncler, Brunello Cucinelli and Ferragamo. The Italian Trade Agency brought 77 brands to the Coterie trade show in September 2026.

  • Retail distress is reshaping the customer base

    Saks Global, owner of Saks, Neiman Marcus and Bergdorf Goodman, filed for Chapter 11 in January 2026. CaaStle went into Chapter 7 and Showfields closed, while Bluecore and Syrup Tech were sold. Startups selling to department stores face concentrated risk.

  • World-class schools, shrinking pipeline

    FIT and Parsons rank first and second in CEOWORLD's 2025 world fashion school ranking, and FIT opened a $188.5M academic building in October 2025. But city fashion completions fell about 30% from 2016 to 2022. No dedicated fashion accelerator in this map shows recent activity since the CFDA Fashion Incubator ended in 2017 and Pratt's closed in 2019.

The city at a glance

New York is the commercial capital of American fashion: headquarters, showrooms, buyers, media and schools. Its manufacturing base has largely gone.

What the numbers say

  • Commercial, not industrial. About 130,000 fashion jobs remain in the city, but only about 5,700 are in apparel manufacturing.
  • Luxury real estate. Kering, Prada and Rolex have committed about $2B to buying or building on Fifth Avenue since 2023.
  • Talent at risk. Fashion program completions in the city fell about 30% between 2016 and 2022.
  • City support is small. The Fashion Manufacturing Initiative has granted $6.7M since 2013; the new Local Production Fund adds $1.7M over two years.

What the schools offer innovators

  • FIT · DTech Lab (with Infor) runs industry projects with paid student researchers; the Joyce F. Brown Academic Building, opened October 2025, holds the largest campus knitting and weaving lab in the US.
  • Parsons · Entrepreneurs Lab (ELab) for design-led ventures; strong international pull.
  • Pratt · ran the Brooklyn Fashion + Design Accelerator, which launched 37 businesses before closing in 2019.
  • LIM College · acquired by the Japan Educational Foundation (owner of ESMOD Paris) in December 2025; MBA in luxury brand management.
  • Cornell Tech Runway · more than 120 companies; no fashion startups in the latest cohort.

Method and limits

  • Scope. Companies innovating in fashion, luxury, beauty and jewelry, with headquarters or their primary US office in the New York metro area, that are public or have raised at least $1M or earned at least $1M in revenue. Digital-native brands are included when their model, not just their product, is new.
  • Not a full register. The census profiles 69 companies chosen for funding, visibility and recent activity. Research ran against a fixed search budget and was interrupted once by a service limit; 92 names reviewed and left out are listed below with the reason.
  • Method. Seven research desks (commerce and AI; circularity, materials and supply chain; beauty, jewelry and brands; programs and investors; schools; incumbents; city indicators) plus a gap-filling pass built lists from investor portfolios, funding trackers and trade press, and checked each record against primary or dated sources.
  • Verification. Before publication, independent reviewers re-checked 279 figures and statements against primary sources: 177 confirmed, 48 corrected and 54 could not be verified. Corrections are applied throughout, and checked companies are marked.
  • Confidence. 5 company profiles are rated high and 12 low. Low means the headquarters or the funding rests on one aggregator or on prior knowledge. Low-confidence rows are labelled.
  • Gaps. Founding year is missing for 17 companies and total raised for 36. Revenue is disclosed by only 7. Private brands rarely publish figures.
  • Conflicting figures. City fashion employment ranges from 110,000 to 180,000 depending on definition and year; state figures (312,000 jobs) cover a wider industry. The page names the source next to each figure.
  • Dates. Research closed October 19, 2026. Check the linked sources before acting on a record.
